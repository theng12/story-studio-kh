# Backyard Naturalist preview provenance

Seven previews are declared by two of this collection's five standalone Content Packs. **All seven now exist**, rendered on 2026-09-09 under the owner's authorisation of that date. This file records why, exactly what was run to produce them, and the prompt text of each, so a later reader can reproduce the run rather than guess at it.

The three packs that declare no raster assets — `com.storystudio.topic.backyard-creatures`, `com.storystudio.topic.backyard-plants` and `com.storystudio.metadata-formula.backyard-sleep-titles` — are complete: they build deterministically, sign, and pass `validateContentPack` under an ephemeral authoring key. The other two were **blocked on previews only** and are no longer blocked; their documents already validate against the signed Content Pack schema, which was checked directly by building each of them once in a throwaway directory under `/tmp` with disposable rasters in place. Those rasters were deleted and no placeholder byte was written into this repository.

## Three previews per visual style

The owner's rule, set on 2026-09-08 and applied to Royal History first, carries forward here: every Visual Style declares **three** previews rather than one, and the three are chosen so a buyer sees the style where it is easy, where it is quiet, and where it is hard.

- **hero** — the shot the style exists for, the one a cover would use.
- **scene** — a quiet wide page with **no creature in it at all**, so a buyer can see what the look does when nothing is moving. For a field-guide style whose subject is always one living thing, the honest empty case is a plant specimen alone on the paper: no animal, no insect, no eye anywhere in the frame.
- **face** — the hardest case the style can be asked for, chosen inside the style's own contract rather than against it.
  - Naturalist Sketchbook exists to build every value out of fine cross-hatching on bright cream paper, staying high-key throughout, with a light warm tint that never becomes the drawing. Its hardest case is therefore a small animal's face at close crop: at that range there is no silhouette to rescue the drawing, the paper still has to be the lightest thing in the picture, and every value on a curved, hairy, many-eyed head has to come from the spacing and crossing of pen strokes alone, with no wash to hide behind and no dark ground to sit against. If the look survives one face at that range, it survives everything else the recipe will ask of it.

The Thumbnail Recipe declares four, as the Lost Voyage, Royal History, Golden Epic, Midnight Archives, Sleep Theories and Gory Cartoon History recipes did, so that the fixed frame is shown against four different subjects and a buyer can see what actually varies between episodes. Two of the four carry a creature and two carry a plant, and the marker phrases run from two words to four, so a buyer can see the frame hold at both ends of its own rule.

## Under what authorisation these were rendered

`docs/VISUAL_STYLE_CONTENT_PACK_FORMAT.md` and the `story-studio-visual-style-adoption` skill both state that this control Mac verifies preview handoffs and does not perform production image generation, and that previews come only from owner-supplied original bytes or a production-preview handoff. That standing rule is lifted only by an explicit owner authorisation given in chat for one named pass, as it was for the earlier collections on 2026-09-08. **This collection's previews were rendered under such an authorisation, given on 2026-09-09**, which named the model (`gpt-image-2` through GenStudio, no other route), named this run id, set a credit ceiling for the whole pass, and instructed that the results be published without a further confirmation. Nothing was substituted and no placeholder byte was ever written to let a pack pass validation dishonestly: the runs below are the only source of these seven files.

## The rejected first returns, kept as history

`naturalist-sketchbook-face.webp` was rendered three times and the first two returns were **thrown away rather than committed**. Both drew the jumping spider with **six eyes** — the two large principal eyes, the small pair beside them and the pair on top of the head — where this collection's acceptance rules demand the count be right or the image be rejected: "an image with the wrong number of legs, wings, eyes or leaflets for the species it names must be rejected rather than committed."

The second return, under `run_id` `backyard-naturalist-previews-1-retry2` (job `342d7222`), was submitted with the prompt completely unchanged, on the assumption that the first was model drift. It came back with the same six eyes. That is what showed the real cause, and it was **an error in the prompt rather than in the model**: the original wording enumerated three pairs and then asserted a total of eight.

```text
…the two enormous forward-facing principal eyes low and central, a second pair of small eyes set wide beside them and a further pair further back on the top of the head, eight in total and no more…
```

Two plus two plus two is six. The model drew what was described and ignored the arithmetic, twice, which is the correct reading of a self-contradictory instruction. A jumping spider's fourth pair — the tiny posterior median eyes, set high between the front pair and the rear pair — was simply missing from the list.

So the prompt was corrected rather than resubmitted a third time unchanged, and the correction is the smallest one that makes the sentence true: the missing pair was named and the total restated as four pairs.

```text
…the two enormous forward-facing principal eyes low and central, a second pair of small eyes set wide beside them, a third and much smaller pair set high between those two pairs, and a fourth pair further back on the top of the head, four pairs and eight eyes in total and no more…
```

The corrected prompt is the one recorded as Prompt 3 below and the one this collection ships. It was run once, under `run_id` `backyard-naturalist-previews-1-fix3` (job `d15cb6c2`), and returned all eight eyes in four pairs at the first attempt, with the single oversized googly eye pasted over the leftmost rear eye exactly as the prompt asks. Nothing else in the wording was touched.

The lesson for a re-run, and the reason this is written out rather than quietly fixed: **an anatomy prompt in this collection must enumerate the parts it counts.** Stating a total the enumeration does not reach buys two rejected images at 29 credits each before anybody notices the sentence is wrong.

## Acceptance rules specific to this collection

Original subjects only. Every creature and plant in these prompts is described from scratch as an ordinary member of a real species, and must be drawn from the description; a returned image that reproduces a recognisable character, a real person, a company mark, an invented insignia presented as genuine, or a copied composition must be rejected rather than committed. No human being, no hand and no bare skin appears in any prompt in this collection, and a returned image containing any of them must be rejected rather than committed. Anatomy is the product here: an image with the wrong number of legs, wings, eyes or leaflets for the species it names must be rejected rather than committed, however well it draws. Every subject must be alive and undamaged — an image showing a creature dead, pinned, squashed, injured or being killed must be rejected rather than committed. The look is high-key throughout: an image that arrives dim, lamplit, vignetted or set against a dark ground has broken the style's central promise and must be rejected rather than committed. In the three style previews no word may appear anywhere in the frame at all. In the four recipe previews the marker phrase must be spelled exactly as written, unclipped, in the stated number of lines, and it must be the only text in the picture; there is no scale bar, no label, no caption and no second phrase.

## Prompt 1 — `naturalist-sketchbook-hero.webp` (style preview, hero, no text)

> A naturalist's field-guide illustration in pen and ink on bright cream paper, sixteen by nine, drawn rather than rendered, and high-key throughout so the paper stays the lightest thing in the picture. One subject only: a single garden snail, whole, seen from slightly above and to the side, placed a little left of centre and low in the frame, at rest with its body extended and both pairs of tentacles up. Anatomy is scientifically accurate: a coiled shell of four and a half whorls turning to the right, a muscular foot with its fringed edge, a breathing pore on the right side of the mantle, two long upper tentacles carrying the eyes and two short lower ones. Contours are drawn once, confidently, with a fine nib in a thin dark brown-black ink of slightly living weight, a touch heavier where a form turns away and lighter where it turns into the light, and a few construction marks are left visible rather than erased. Every value is built from fine cross-hatching: parallel strokes at a shallow angle for the first tone, a second crossed set for the second, a third only in the deepest crevices, with the stroke direction following the surface — around the whorls of the shell, along the length of the foot. Nothing is smudged, blended, stippled into softness or filled solid black. Over the ink lies one light warm wash of thin honeyed ochre applied in a few broad transparent passes, warming the drawing without ever becoming it. The paper is bare cream everywhere else: more than half the frame carries nothing at all, the page is flat and frontal, and there is no horizon, no ground line, no scenery, no background object, no cast shadow and no vignette. Texture is the faint tooth of good cream paper and nothing else.
